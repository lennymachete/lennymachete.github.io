Building a Decision System from Scratch

Leonard Mazzotti · March 2026 · lennymachete.github.io
VERSION 5.0

I was juggling contract work, building new skills, and trying to figure out what came next after 15 years in action sports. I had no infrastructure for any of it. No system, no process, no way to evaluate what was worth my time and what wasn't. So I built one.

Nobody asked me to do it. There was no manager, no requirement, no deadline. I just knew that making decisions one at a time with no framework was costing me hours I didn't have. So I designed a structured evaluation and routing system that could handle volume, make consistent decisions, and tell me why something failed when it did.

It's on version 5 now. Version 1 had seven categories and took too long to do anything useful. Every version since has been about stripping out what didn't work and making the logic cleaner. It's still not done. Systems aren't supposed to be done. They're supposed to get better.

Here's how it works, what broke, and what I changed.

System architecture: v5.0 — two-lane triage, five-check evaluation with override logic, sequential gating pipeline

The Problem with No System

Before I built anything, I was looking at everything the same way. Every opportunity got the same amount of attention whether it was a real fit or a complete waste of time. I had no criteria. No documentation. No way to look back at a decision and figure out why I made it.

What happens when you work like that is pretty predictable. You burn hours on things that were never going to work. You miss the stuff that actually fits because you're buried in stuff that doesn't. And when something fails, you can't figure out why because you never wrote down the reasoning. You're just guessing every time and hoping you guess right.

I needed something that did three things: kill the noise early, evaluate what's left with the same criteria every time, and keep a record so I could learn from what didn't work.

Two-Lane Architecture

The first real design decision was triage. Not everything belongs in the same pipeline. I split everything into two tracks based on what I was actually trying to accomplish. Track 1 handles immediate needs. Track 2 handles longer-term targets. If something doesn't fit either track, it's dead before I spend any time on it.

Version 1 had seven lanes. That was way overbuilt. More categories didn't make the decisions better. They just made everything slower and harder to keep consistent because I was managing too many definitions at once. Collapsing to two lanes was the single biggest improvement across all five versions. I keep coming back to this: simpler structure with clear rules beats complex structure every time. Every time.

The Evaluation Framework

The evaluation wasn't just about filtering. It was about learning. Every item I processed taught me something, whether it was a strong match, a stretch, or a reject.

Strong matches showed me what my actual skill set looked like when it aligned with what someone needed. Stretches showed me where the gaps were and what I'd need to build. Rejects were the most useful. When something didn't fit, I didn't just move on. I asked why. What keywords were showing up in roles that weren't right for me? What framing was I missing? What patterns kept leading to dead ends?

That's how the system got smarter over time. It wasn't just filtering volume. It was translating my own experience back to me.

That was the part I didn't expect. When I started building this, I couldn't describe half of what I'd done in professional terms. I'd spent 15 years in action sports working across marketing, community operations, brand partnerships, event management, and athlete development. I knew I'd done the work. I just didn't have the language for it yet.

Every evaluation forced me to compare what a role needed against what I'd actually done, and that comparison built the vocabulary. Strong matches told me which bullets were already there. Stretches told me what to frame honestly. Rejects told me what to stop chasing.

The system didn't just help me make decisions. It helped me understand what I was actually bringing to the table.

The Process

Every item that passes evaluation moves through a set pipeline. Discovery, evaluation, variant selection, tailoring, gap audit, pre-submission check, execution. Same order every time. No steps get skipped.

The gap audit is where the system forces you to be honest with yourself. For every requirement, the question is simple: do I have this experience or not? If I have it but haven't written it down yet, I translate it into the right language. If I partially have it, I frame it honestly. If I don't have it, I leave the gap. I don't fill it with something that isn't real.

That rule is built into the system on purpose. The moment you start making things up to fill gaps, every decision after that is built on bad information. The whole framework falls apart. Honest inputs are the only thing that makes the rest of it work.

What Broke and What I Changed

The biggest failure wasn't a design flaw. It was me reading the situation wrong.

I lost two opportunities I thought I was well positioned for. My first instinct was that something was wrong with how I was presenting myself. So I started tearing apart my positioning and rebuilding it.

That was the wrong fix. When I went back and actually looked at what happened, the problem was timing. Both of those went to people who got supplementary materials in front of the hiring manager before the first screen even happened. My positioning was fine. I was just too slow.

That one diagnostic produced a new rule: get a role-specific document out the same day as the application. Before any phone screen. Not after. That change came directly from the system doing what it's supposed to do. When something fails, the documentation lets you trace back to where it actually went wrong instead of guessing and rebuilding the wrong thing.

Version 5 exists because of that failure. And the next version will exist because of whatever version 5 gets wrong. That's the whole point.

Version History

Version What Changed Why
v1.0 Seven evaluation lanes. Every item reviewed against all seven categories. Initial build. Tried to cover every possible scenario upfront.
v2.0 Consolidated to four lanes. Added priority classification to triage. Seven was too slow. Couldn't keep definitions consistent across that many categories.
v3.0 Introduced the five-check evaluation and override rule. Needed a repeatable scoring method that handled edge cases without creating new lanes.
v4.0 Collapsed to two lanes. Added gap audit and sequential gating. Four lanes still created routing confusion. Two tracks with clear criteria eliminated ambiguity.
v5.0 Added same-day deployment rule. Diagnostic framework for failures. Misread two losses as positioning failures. Actual cause: timing. System now traces failures to root cause.

Why I Build This Way

I think about systems the same way I think about communities. It's never about the single decision. It's about building something that makes every decision after it better.

When I ran communities for brands like Sun Bum, Volcom, and Red Bull, the work wasn't the individual interactions. It was building the structure that made thousands of interactions consistent and trustworthy over years. Sun Bum doesn't tell people to wear their sunscreen. They tell people to wear sunscreen. That's the difference between something that serves itself and something that actually means something.

I build systems the same way. Not because someone told me to. Because that's how I think.
